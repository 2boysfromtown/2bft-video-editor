# Director/Editor Method: the deeper layer
*Research synthesis, 2026-10-25. This file assumes the existing method file (3-second hook, one subject and one turn, three sound layers, two silences, J/L cuts, cue map and beat grid, pace variation, reading time, camera-move variety, one signature shot, review loop). It only adds what that file is missing.*

**Tags.** **[S]** means the claim or number comes from a cited source. **[H]** means a practitioner rule of thumb or my own derivation: use it as a starting value and adjust it in review. Most numbers below are [H] or come from vendor blogs, because pro editors rarely publish frame counts. Treat them as defaults, not laws.

---

## 0. Which rule wins when rules conflict

1. **Truth and safety.** Never make someone appear to react to something they didn't react to. Stay under the flash limits (§5.5).
2. **Emotion.** What should the viewer feel at this moment?
3. **Story and progression.** Did the viewer just learn or gain something new?
4. **Rhythm.**
5. **Eye-trace and legibility.** Can the viewer find the point and read it?
6. **Polish and continuity.** Spatial match, screen direction, clean frames.

This is Murch's Rule of Six restated for this job. His weights are emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, 2D screen plane 5%, 3D space 4%. When a cut can't satisfy everything, give up items from the bottom of the list first [S: UT Austin wiki, Artlist]. Murch also counts emotion as worth more than the other five together.

## 1. Deciding each cut

### 1.1 Score every cut with the Rule of Six
- **Before you lock a cut**, run six quick checks. (1) Does the emotion carry across the cut? (2) Does it move the story forward? (3) Is it on the rhythm? (4) Will the eye land where the new shot's point is? (5) Is screen direction kept, for example the cursor moving right before and after? (6) Is spatial logic intact? If check 1 or 2 fails, the cut is wrong, even if 3 to 6 pass.
- **When the best emotional take has a continuity flaw** (headphones moved, a different window open), keep the take and hide the flaw with a punch-in or an insert. Schoonmaker says the best performance take matters more than matching details. When a clean line competes with a laugh, she says "we would always go for the laugh" (Thelma Schoonmaker, Film Comment) [S].

### 1.2 Where exactly to cut
- **Cut when the thought is complete, not when the sentence is.** Murch's blink idea: a listener blinks at the moment they understand an idea, and a cut is that blink [S: Musicbed]. In talking-head footage, natural cut points are the creator's own blinks, a breath, a glance away from the lens, or a head turn after a point lands.
- **Watch the eyes, and give the emotion its time.** Tony Zhou's essay on how editors think: the eyes show the emotion, and the editor decides how long to stay on a face before and after a line. The moments to hunt for are the frames where you can see a thought change [S: Kottke on Every Frame a Painting]. Rule: after a punchline or a realisation, hold the face for at least the length of the reaction, usually 10 to 30 frames, before the next cut [H].
- **Cut on movement to hide the cut.** Kurosawa often cut during movement, because the eye follows the motion and doesn't notice the edit. For screen and webcam footage, movement means a hand gesture, a head turn, the cursor flying to a button, or a window opening. Make the cut on that motion [S: EFAP Kurosawa transcript].
- **Reset the rhythm with stillness.** Kurosawa often ended a scene on something static and then cut straight into movement, so the viewer can't predict the next cut [S]. Rule: after a fast run of cuts, land on a 1.5 to 3 second still shot (a held face or a frozen screen), then hit the next segment in motion.
- **Eye-trace across cuts.** Keep the main subject within about 10% of its previous position in frame. Don't cut between two shots of the same size; skip a size, for example wide to tight [S: AIR Media-Tech, practitioner]. On screen recordings this means the next zoom target should sit near where the cursor or the viewer's eye already is. If the point jumps across the frame, lead the eye there with an arrow or the cursor first.

### 1.3 Meaning comes from what sits next to what
- **The Kuleshov effect.** The same neutral face reads as hunger, grief or desire depending on the shot that follows it (soup, a coffin, a woman) [S: WJEC, Futility Closet]. In reaction-cam editing the stimulus shot decides the emotion. So **put the reaction directly after the true stimulus**, which should be on screen for at least its reading time. **Never** move a reaction onto a different stimulus to fake a bigger response. That breaks rule 0.1 and the trust of a live-stream audience.
- **Mamet's uninflected images.** Tell the story by placing plain shots side by side and let the cut carry the meaning [S: On Directing Film blurbs, alex4d]. Rule: **if the cut already says it, the graphic shouldn't repeat it.** For example, the error is on screen, then his face drops. Don't also add a "FAIL" sticker unless that's the comic beat.

### 1.4 Eisenstein's five montage types as tools [S: StudioBinder, PremiumBeat]
| Type | What drives the cut | Use in this creator's videos |
|---|---|---|
| Metric | Fixed shot lengths, whatever the content | Build-progress montage: 8 or 12 frames per shot on the beat grid |
| Rhythmic | Movement inside the shot sets cut length | Cut when typing stops, the cursor lands or a hand slams down |
| Tonal | Mood (light, colour, shape) | A late-night debugging run: blue cast, dimmer, slower |
| Overtonal | All three combined | Save it for the climax: metric beat, rhythmic motion and tonal shift together |
| Intellectual | Two unrelated images make an idea | "3 hours by hand" shot, then "AI, 40 seconds" shot. The idea forms in the viewer's head |

Rule: **each montage in a video should knowingly be one of these types.** A montage that is none of them is just clips in a row.

### 1.5 Habits of named editors, as rules
- **Dede Allen (Bonnie and Clyde).** She was among the first in Hollywood to open sequences on close-ups or jump cuts and to run the next shot's sound under the current picture. Her "shock cutting" married jump cuts to classical cutting [S: NPR, Encyclopedia.com]. Rule: **open a segment on its most telling close detail** (the red error line, his hand freezing), not an establishing wide shot. Widen later if needed.
- **Sally Menke (Tarantino).** She came from documentary, set the script aside and cut from the footage as it actually exists. She let long takes play while tension was building, and her cuts land on specific movements that match the music rather than on every beat [S: Below the Line interview, RogerEbert.com, Videomaker]. Rule: **while tension is rising** (will the build compile?), stop cutting. Hold one long take of 6 to 15 seconds, then release it with a cut on the payoff movement.
- **Edgar Wright's visual comedy, eight devices** [S: Every Frame a Painting via Laughing Squid and the Funny How Substack]: things entering or leaving frame in funny ways; "there and back again" movement; matched transitions between scenes; the perfectly timed sound effect; action synced to music; dramatic lighting cues; fence gags (the reveal); and imaginary gunfights. He also uses very fast montages of mundane actions: swipe transitions and extreme close-ups of ordinary objects [S: Little White Lies]. Applied here:
  - Match cut on shape or motion: laptop lid closes to a black frame, or a mouse click becomes a door slam to the next segment.
  - Hyper-montage of a routine step (open the terminal, paste, Enter, coffee sip): 5 to 8 extreme close-ups of 4 to 8 frames each, each with its own short sound. Use it to compress setup that would otherwise be dead time [H for the frame counts].
  - Lighting cue: when he realises something, darken the background by 20 to 30% over 6 frames [H].
- **Murch's Law of Two and a Half (sound).** An audience can follow about two to two-and-a-half similar sounds at once. Beyond that, they merge into texture [S: Krotos interview]. Rule: in a fast insert montage, **don't stack more than two distinct whooshes at once**. Make the rest one shared texture.
- **The editor stands in for the audience.** Murch has described the editor as the audience's only representative on the crew [S: Cinephilia Beyond]. Rule: log your **first-viewing** reactions (where you laughed, got bored or got confused) before you get used to the footage. You only see it fresh once.

### 1.6 Cut-rate reference points [S]
- Murch: a dialogue scene still feels normal at about **6 cuts a minute**; a convincing action sequence runs about **25** [S: Musicbed].
- MrBeast, mid-video minute: **38 cuts** in a 2023 video against **23 cuts** in the 2024 abandoned-city video, after he said publicly that he'd slowed down, let scenes breathe and yelled less. Views went up, but the cause isn't proven [S: Tubefilter].
- So: **use cut rate as an output of the content, not a target.** High-energy segments can reach 20 to 40 cuts a minute; explanation and payoff segments can drop to 4 to 10 [H].

## 2. The director's pass, before touching the timeline

### 2.1 Questions to answer in writing for every video
1. **What is this about emotionally, as opposed to what happens?** Lumet's first decision for any film [S: The Script Blog quoting *Making Movies*]. Example: "A builder racing his own doubt", not "I built an agent".
2. **Now that I know that, how should I tell it?** Lumet's second decision. Theme sets the casting, shooting, editing and scoring choices [S: No Film School].
3. **Spine.** Kazan found the core of each script and wrote it down [S: Penguin Random House listing]. Write it as one verb phrase, such as "to prove it works live". Every segment must serve that verb, or it gets cut.
4. **Mamet's three questions, for each segment.** Who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why now? A scene ends when the attempt resolves [S: Mamet's memo to the writers of *The Unit*, Slashfilm]. Rule: **cut out of a segment the moment the want resolves.** Everything after that is the tail.
5. **What is the turn?** Name the value change in each segment (works → broken, confused → clear). A segment with no turn gets compressed into montage or cut.
6. **Where is the subtext?** In live footage, look for the gap between what he says and what is happening ("this will work first try" while the error log scrolls). That irony is the best material in a stream. Cut hard to the proof.
7. **What is the "wow" no one else could show?** (§3.2)
8. **What is the packaging promise?** (§3.1) Every edit decision must deliver that promise.

### 2.2 Theme drives style: a "frame plot"
In *12 Angry Men*, Lumet planned a lens progression. He moved from about 28–40 mm toward 50, 75 and 100 mm, and lowered the camera over the film, so the room seemed to close in on the jurors [S: Slashfilm quoting *Making Movies*; BFI]. Do the same with framing and graphics across a video:
- **Pressure or deadline videos:** start wide, airy and graphically spacious. Get tighter as stakes rise (base framing 100% → 108% → 115%), cut faster, let the timer graphic grow. At the payoff, release: go wide, hold the shot, open up the music.
- **Discovery or explainer videos:** start tight and confused (close shots, cluttered screen), then open up as things become clear (clean full-screen diagram, steady wide face).
Write the frame plot in the cue map before cutting [H].

### 2.3 Cut the radio version first
For interview and talking-head material, the usual documentary method is to build the story from the audio or transcript first, then cover it with B-roll and graphics [S: Creative COW, Frame.io]. Kurzgesagt also locks narration first, and the voiceover sets the animation timing [S: kurzgesagt.org]. Rule: **lock the radio cut (story order, trims, rough pacing) before adding any graphic.** Graphics are timed to the voice, never the other way round.

### 2.4 Coverage check for screen + webcam footage
List every "angle" you actually have:
- screen capture
- webcam
- any phone or B-cam
- chat log
- terminal and browser history
- generated outputs (images, files)
- timestamps and markers

Then answer three questions [H]:
- Which beats have **proof** on screen?
- Which have only **claims**?
- Which emotional beats have **no face coverage**? For example, the face was hidden behind a full-screen browser. Those beats need a reaction from just before or after, or a sound-led treatment.

## 3. Structure for retention in long-form (10–15 min)

### 3.1 Packaging comes before the edit
- Galloway calls YouTube a game of clicks and watches and puts the click at about 40% of the outcome. His other guidance:
  - Titles around 50 characters, sometimes 60, simple enough for non-native English readers. That matters for a Hinglish audience.
  - Thumbnails are about 80–90% click psychology and 10–20% design. They must pass a 1–2 second glance test.
  - Thumbnail text of 2–4 words, never more than 5–6.
  - Sketch about 10 concepts and finish 3 that are genuinely different, not three colour variants.
  - Write and record 3–4 intro versions and pick the best [S: Creator Science podcast].
- **The first frames must continue the thumbnail.** MrBeast's guide says mismatches between thumbnail and video (colour, size, location) make viewers leave, and that the title and thumbnail set expectations the video must meet [S: MrBeast memo]. Rule: the opening 1–3 seconds should show the thumbnail's subject in the same state the thumbnail promised.
- Veritasium's view is that packaging largely decides success or failure. Third-party summaries describe his split between enticing-but-accurate "legit bait" and misleading "click traps" [S: veritasium.com, plus secondary summaries]. Rule: **the title asks the question and the video answers it fully.**

### 3.2 The minute map (MrBeast's guide, scaled to 12 minutes) [S: MrBeast memo]
| Window | MrBeast's guide | Scaled rule for this creator |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:00 | Most viewers leave here. Match the packaging, front-load the best material | Prove the promise. Show the end result or the stakes |
| 1:00–3:00 | Stop telling people what they'll see and start showing them. Use "crazy progression" | Compress the setup, e.g. "4 hours of setup in 20 seconds" |
| ~3:00 | Re-engagement: a spectacle beat | First big demo or "wow" |
| 3:00–6:00 | The most exciting material, kept simple | The core build and the first failure |
| ~6:00 | Second re-engagement that pushes the story forward | Midpoint turn: a new problem or twist |
| 6:00–end | Viewers past here are invested and will accept longer explanation. A weak back half kills videos | The deep "why", then escalation to the payoff |
| End | End abruptly after the payoff. Don't signal the ending early | Payoff, then out within 5–15 seconds into the end screen |

Other rules from the guide: raise the stakes step by step ("stair-stepping"), and protect the "wow factor", the thing no other channel could do. A viewer who watches half the video very likely finishes it.

**Galloway's 30-second test:** if the first ~30 seconds of a long-form video couldn't stand alone as a Short, the intro is weak [S: interview summary]. YouTube's own "Intro" metric is the share of viewers still watching at 30 seconds. YouTube suggests checking the title and thumbnail match and revising the opening when it's low [S: YouTube Help].

### 3.3 Pace is new information, not cut speed
- Mario Joos, a former MrBeast retention strategist, says pacing comes from **how fast you introduce new information that moves the story**, not from how fast you cut [S: Vidpros on Mario Joos]. He also suggests:
  - Tag each element as **progressive** (moves toward the goal) or **non-progressive**. Jokes are non-progressive; allow about one per segment, never several in a row.
  - Always tell viewers where they are in the structure ("step 2 of 3"). He rates this highly.
  - Run the **replicability test**: could another smart person make this without doing the research? If so, it needs more depth.
  - Judge retention against similar videos in the niche. He calls a fixed 70% retention target a myth.
- **Fatigue.** In 2024 MrBeast publicly dropped the "ultra fast paced/overstim" style and said it doesn't even work [S: Tubefilter]. Rule: **if a segment needs effects to feel alive, the segment is the problem.** Fix it by compressing or cutting it, not by adding effects.
- Floors and ceilings from practitioners [S: AIR Media-Tech]:
  - No static talking head or single screen frame for more than about 40 seconds.
  - About 2 seconds with no motion and no sound reads as the video ending, so avoid dead air unless it's a deliberate silence.
  - Hold about 1 second of near-silence before a high-energy shift.
  - Save heavy transitions for scene changes, reveals and peaks; plain cuts everywhere else.

### 3.4 Open loops, re-hooks and structural context [H, drawn from MrBeast and Joos]
- **Never have zero open loops.** Before closing a question, open the next one ("it works, but the cost was ₹____").
- About every 2–3 minutes, close one loop, show the progress marker (chapter n of N), and open the next.
- Re-hook types, rotated:
  - a direct question
  - a specific tease (use sparingly)
  - a pattern break: new layout, location or angle, or dropping the music
  - a visible counter or timer jumping

### 3.5 Reading the retention graph [S: YouTube Help]
- **Spikes** are rewatched sections. They can mean delight, **or that the section was unclear.** Check which before copying it.
- **Dips** are skipped or abandoned sections. Look at the 10 seconds before each one.
- If **top moments** come late, move similar material earlier.
- **Don't panic over micro-dips** if the segment adds lasting value. Galloway's example kept about 98% through a dip [S: interview summary].

### 3.6 Chapters and end screens [S: YouTube Help]
- **Chapters:** the first timestamp must be 00:00, with at least 3 chapters in ascending order, each at least 10 seconds long. Design chapter cards to match the description timestamps.
- **End screens** go in the last 5–20 seconds, and the video must be at least 25 seconds long. Keep that window free of important graphics where end-screen elements sit, and end on a useful frame (the result) rather than black.

### 3.7 Explainers: state the misconception first
Muller's PhD research found that clear expository science videos felt clear but taught little, because viewers kept their wrong ideas. Videos that stated the misconception and then fixed it felt more confusing, yet test scores roughly doubled [S: THE Journal, MetaFilter]. Rule: **for any "how AI does X" explanation, say the common wrong belief out loud first** ("most people think the model remembers you..."), then break it on screen.

## 4. Turning a stream or VOD into a YouTube video

### 4.1 Mindset and ratios
- **The stream is the shoot. The video is the film.** A 3–4 hour stream usually becomes a 10–15 minute video, so you keep about 5–8% of it [H]. Length follows the story: don't stretch an 8-minute story to 10 [S: StreamHub].
- One stream can also become 1–2 videos of 5–10 minutes on specific segments, plus Shorts [S: Streamerplus].

### 4.2 Logging pass: what to look for
Make one fast pass and mark timestamps for:
1. **Chat spikes.** Bursts in message rate and emote spam help predict highlights; there is research on this from Twitch broadcasts [S: Fu et al., EMNLP 2017].
2. **Loudness and laughter peaks:** the top ~5–10% of short-term loudness against his own baseline for that stream [H].
3. **Turns:** the first error, the first success, "wait, what?", silence followed by an outburst.
4. **Irony setups:** confident claims made before failures. Pair them with the failure.
5. **Proof frames:** the moment the output appears (a generated image, a passing test, a deployed URL).
6. **External events:** donations, raids, guests, hard questions from chat.
7. **Live markers** placed during the stream, if any [S: YouTube Help, Eklipse].
Then turn the marks into a beat list. Each beat is one line: want → obstacle → result.

### 4.3 Templates for turning a VOD into a story
- **A. Cold-open quest** (the default) [S: StreamHub, plus H]: cold open on the climax (the last ~10 seconds of the peak) → jump back ("3 hours earlier") → a context bridge of **30 seconds or less** stating the goal and stakes. StreamHub warns that a setup running around 5 minutes loses viewers → escalate with small wins and setbacks → return to the cold-open moment with full context → a last joke or one-line CTA instead of a fade out. The first 5 seconds must make a promise; never open on "welcome back".
- **B. Story circle (Harmon):** you, need, go, search, find, take, return, change [S: Spines]. For a build stream: comfort → a problem he needs to solve → starting the build → trial and error → it works → the cost (bug, bill, limitation) → shipping → what he learned or how he changed.
- **C. Clock challenge:** "Can AI build X in 60 minutes?" A visible timer is the progress bar and the stakes at once. Cut the beats to the clock, with stair-stepped difficulty.
- **D. Build log in chapters** (for teaching streams): problem → plan → build montage → bug → fix → demo → takeaways. Each chapter is a Mamet unit with its own want.
- **E. Best-of countdown:** good for comedy streams, weakest for story. Use it only when no arc exists.

### 4.4 Dead time, chat and inside jokes
- **Waiting** (installs, renders, AI generating): speed it up 4–16× under a small timer, with ticking or whirring sound, or replace it with a 1–2 second "later" card. Long stretches of base-building can become a 30-second montage [S: StreamHub for the montage idea; H for the speeds].
- **Cut** greetings, reading out chat names, tech fiddling and housekeeping [S: StreamHub].
- **Keep chat only when it causes a turn** (a question that changes the plan, a heckle he reacts to). Show the message as a clean card **before** the reaction; that is Kuleshov again. Blur or trim usernames of private viewers unless the creator wants shout-outs [H].
- **Inside jokes:** a highlight has to work for a stranger. Add a one-line overlay that explains the reference, or cut it [S: StreamHub].
- **Audio:** mute the stream's background music and score it fresh in the edit. Compress the voice more than the live mix [S: StreamHub].

### 4.5 Reaction-cam and screen grammar [H]
- **Stimulus first, reaction second.** Show the screen event full and legible, then cut or zoom to the face for the reaction. Never put both at the same size when one of them is the point.
- Picture-in-picture face: about 22–30% of frame width in 16:9, in the corner the cursor isn't using. **Move or resize the facecam when it covers UI** [S: StreamHub].
- On a big reaction, go **face full-frame** with a punch-in, then return to the PiP layout.

### 4.6 AI-building and coding streams specifically [H; observed in the Fireship, ThePrimeagen and Theo styles, not from published guides]
- **Code is the action.** Zoom to the 5–15 lines that matter, highlight the changed line, and dim the rest by 40–60%.
- Terminal errors get a red underline or box callout. The "it works" moment gets a full-screen demo with no PiP.
- When prompting an AI live, show the prompt as a clean card (retyped or cropped). Speed through the stream of generated text, then **hold** on the key output.
- Show the costs (tokens, ₹, minutes) as running counters. They are natural stakes.
- Fireship-style narration-led pacing (memes as punctuation, rapid code inserts) suits recap segments. Theo and Prime-style long reaction takes suit the opinion segments.

## 5. Effects that react to energy

### 5.1 The principle
Effects **confirm** energy that is already in the performance; they never create it. Every shake, punch or flash needs a trigger in the footage: a loud transient, a stressed word, a slam, a sudden gesture. Adding energy to a flat moment reads as fake and cheap.

### 5.2 Detecting energy [H]
- Build a per-stream baseline from the creator's own audio: median short-term loudness over windows of about 400 ms, plus median pitch and speech rate.
- Energy score = a weighted z-score of loudness (more than about 6 dB above baseline counts as strong), pitch rise, speech-rate rise, and motion energy in the webcam area (frame difference). Add Hinglish trigger words: "bhai", "dekho", "ho gaya!", "let's go", "kya baat".
- Place the effect on the **onset** of the stressed syllable or the slam (the audio transient), not on the loudness peak.

### 5.3 Shake spec: the "trauma" model from game feel
The game-camera approach traced to Eiserloh's GDC talk works like this [S: kidscancode Godot recipe, Bevy example, npm screen-shake]:
- Each event adds "trauma" on a 0–1 scale, and trauma drains over time.
- The visible shake is trauma squared or cubed. Bevy's note: "Camera shakes don't feel punchy when they go up linearly."
- Motion is sampled from **smooth noise (Perlin or simplex), not per-frame random values**, which looks like teleporting jitter.
- Game defaults are much wilder than video should be. Bevy uses 20 px maximum translation, 10° maximum angle and a decay of 0.5 per second. The Godot recipe uses up to 100×75 px and 0.1 rad.
- Vlambeer's pistol shot: the camera is kicked back 6 px and shake is raised by 4, then decays quickly [S: Infovore quoting Nijman].
- Video needs **shorter decay and much less rotation**, because the face and text must stay readable.

Starting values for a 1080p frame [H]:
| Level | Trigger | Peak translation | Rotation | Noise frequency | Attack / decay | Extras |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hum (sustained hype for more than 2 s) | energy z ≥ 1.5, sustained | 2–5 px | ≤ 0.3° | 1–3 Hz | 6-frame ramp in / 10-frame release | none |
| L1 Accent | stressed word | no shake | 0 | – | – | Caption pop 100→112→100% over 6 frames, plus a tick SFX |
| L2 Punch | z ≥ 1.5, an exclamation | none, cut-zoom only | 0 | – | instant / hold to the end of the idea | Soft whoosh |
| L3 Hit | z ≥ 2.5, a sharp transient | 8–20 px | ≤ 0.7° | 10–15 Hz | 1 frame / 8–12 frames (≈0.3–0.4 s) | Zoom kick +5–8% that decays, motion blur, low hit |
| L4 Peak (1–2 per video) | the main payoff | 25–45 px | ≤ 2° | 12–18 Hz | 0–1 frame / 15–24 frames | 2–3 frame hit-stop, a 1–2 frame flash at ≤60% opacity, stacked impact SFX |

Implementation notes:
- Scale the shaken layer to 105–110%, or mirror its edges, so borders never show.
- Add directional motion blur matched to shake velocity (180° shutter equivalent) so it reads as a camera, not glitchy pixels.
- In After Effects, keyframe a slider that drives `wiggle(freq, amp)` and animate the amplitude down. A low frequency of about 1 with an amplitude of 10–25 px gives a gentle drift [S: School of Motion]. Impacts need high frequency with quick decay.
- **Shake the footage layer, not the caption or UI layer.** Text the viewer is reading stays still. Captions shake only on the impact word, for 6 frames or less.

### 5.4 Zoom punches and push-ins [H unless marked]
- **Two-size system:** base at 100%, punch at 112–125%. A 1080p webcam in a 1080p timeline gets soft beyond about 120–130%. Go up to 150% only for 4–12 frame hits. 4K sources can go to 200%.
- **Cut-zoom** (instant) is for emphasis and jump-cut cover. **Push-in** (animated over 8–20 frames, ease-out) is for building emotion or a realisation.
- Punch in when the point lands, then **come back out** for the next idea. Zooming in on every line wears viewers out [S: tool vendors, consistent across practitioners]. Never punch on two sentences in a row. Alternate in and out.

### 5.5 Hit-stop, speed ramps, RGB split, flash and impact frames
- **Hit-stop:** freeze the impact frame for 2–4 frames (≈50–130 ms) before the shake starts. Game designers report that 50–100 ms on a heavy hit makes it feel like it connected; too long feels like the game hung [S: Wayline, Bugnet].
- **Speed ramps:** about 2–3 per video, each with a story reason [S: vendor practitioner advice]. The shape is normal → fast lead-in → slow at the decisive moment. On screen recordings, ramp 1× → 8–16× → 1× with eased curves, and replace sped-up audio with a riser or whoosh rather than chipmunk sound [H].
- **RGB split or chromatic aberration:** 2–8 px channel offset for 2–6 frames, only on "machine" moments (the AI thinking, an error, a glitch reveal). It never stays on, and never sits on the face for more than a few frames [H].
- **Flash frames:** 1–2 frames of white or brand colour at 40–70% opacity on a drop or impact [H].
- **Hard safety limit:** no more than **3 flashes in any 1-second window**. Avoid saturated red flashing [S: WCAG 2.3.1]. UK broadcast rules (the Harding test) also cap flash rate at 3 per second and restrict how much of the screen flashes [S: Wikipedia on the Harding test, Pinsent Masons].
- **Impact frames** (anime style): 2–4 frames of a high-contrast, inverted or posterised still at the single biggest moment. Use one per video [H; S for the definition: Know Your Meme].

### 5.6 Syncing to transients
ATSC guidance says sound should never lead picture by more than 15 ms, nor lag it by more than 45 ms. ITU-R BT.1359 puts the detection thresholds at about +45 ms and −125 ms [S: TV Technology, ITU]. One frame is 33 ms at 30 fps and 17 ms at 60 fps. Rule: **put the visual hit on the frame that contains the audio onset, or one frame earlier. Never later.**

### 5.7 Budget and escalation ladder [H]
- **Long-form:** L3 and L4 events average no more than 1 per 45–60 seconds. Total shake time stays at or under 3–5% of runtime. Punches 2–4 per minute in talky sections. Flashes 3 or fewer per video outside montages.
- **Shorts (30–60 s):** at most 3–4 L3 events and 1 L4.
- **Cooldown:** no two L3+ events within 3 seconds of each other, except in a beat-synced montage.
- **Escalation:** the biggest effect is reserved for the biggest moment. If the opening uses L4, the payoff has nowhere to go.

### 5.8 When effects become cheap (any one of these means remove it)
- An effect on almost every sentence, so it no longer signals anything.
- No trigger in the audio or gesture.
- It blurs or shakes information the viewer needs to read (code, UI, captions).
- It lands off the transient or fights the music grid.
- It lands on a serious, sad or quiet beat.
- An effect that stays on constantly (RGB split, glow, permanent hum).
- The same effect at the same strength repeated three times in a row.

## 6. Motion graphics for talking-head and screen videos

### 6.1 The system is what looks professional [H]
- One type family in two weights, 2–3 brand colours plus **one emphasis colour** that only marks the key word or element.
- One animation grammar: elements enter over 8–12 frames with ease-out and leave over 6–8 frames with ease-in. One direction language, for example new things arrive from the right and old ones leave left.
- Rule: **every graphic answers "where should the eye go now?"** Place it where the eye already is (near the face or the cursor), not across the frame.

### 6.2 Lower thirds, chapter cards, progress
- **Lower thirds:** hold 3–6 seconds. One brand standard gives a single-line ID 3 seconds, within 2.5–4 seconds [S: School of Motion, BU brand standards]. Show it only on a person's first appearance.
- **Chapter cards:** 1.5–2.5 seconds, a number plus 2–5 words, matching the YouTube chapters [H].
- **Progress indicator:** a thin segmented bar or "2/5" tag. It gives viewers the structural context Joos stresses [S/H].

### 6.3 UI callouts and screen magnification
- **Prepare the source:** larger fonts (editor at 14 pt or more, terminal at 16 pt or more as a minimum), hide side panels, and turn on VS Code's Screencast Mode so clicks and keystrokes show [S: SSW, DEV Community].
- Check legibility after upload, because platforms downscale [S: Udemy forum].
- **Zoom to the click:** 1.5–2.5× over 12–20 frames with eased movement and a smoothed cursor. That's the grammar tools like Screen Studio automate: auto-zoom on clicks, a gliding cursor, a click ripple [S: Screen Studio guide, Dave Swift review; H for the values].
- **Zoom back out before moving to a distant part of the screen**, so the viewer keeps their bearings.
- **Spotlight:** dim everything else by 40–60%, put a rounded box around the target, and bring an arrow in from empty space. Show keyboard shortcuts as keycap graphics [H].
- **Code legibility for phones:** show no more than about 10–15 lines, with glyph cap height around 30–40 px in a 1080p frame [H].

### 6.4 Captions
- **Long-form:** use emphasis captions for key phrases and numbers, not full subtitles; the YouTube CC track covers accessibility [H].
- **Shorts:** full captions in chunks of 2–4 words for energetic delivery, 2 lines maximum, bold sans-serif with a stroke or plate, contrast of at least 4.5:1, kept in one consistent zone [S: Opus, OpenClip and other vendor guides].
- **Kinetic grammar** [H]: the current word lights up in the emphasis colour; one word per chunk at most gets a scale pop (to 110–115%). Numbers and AI tool names always get the emphasis colour.
- **Hinglish:** no study compares Roman-script and Devanagari captions. Devanagari conjuncts take more vertical space [S: Pictory]. Default to **Roman Hinglish with English tech terms in Latin script**, matching how the audience types comments. A/B test Devanagari on the Hindi-heavy Shorts [H].

### 6.5 B-roll from the footage itself [H]
Use screenshots of outputs, the deployed page, terminal logs, browser tabs, generated images, chat messages, timestamps and cost meters. Give stills a slow 3–5% Ken Burns push and **receipt inserts** (show the proof).

### 6.6 What to borrow from whom
- **Johnny Harris and Vox-style maps:** animated geography with GEOlayers in After Effects; journalism that leads with visuals [S: aescripts]. Use this for AI-pipeline diagrams: node graphs that light up step by step with the narration.
- **Dhruv Rathee-style explainers** (inferred from editors' job posts, not his own account): map and data animation timed to narration, short text callouts, burned-in subtitles [S, weak].
- **Kurzgesagt:** the voice sets the timing, and every video gets its own score [S].
- **Casey Neistat:** shoot far more than you use; find the story in the edit; cut mid-sentence when the audience can infer the rest; open cold, hint at what's coming, then set up [S, secondary].
- **MKBHD, Ali Abdaal, Tech Burner, Satish K, Codebasics:** I found no reliable published process. Treat any "style" claims about them as guesses [noted].

## 7. Shorts and Reels

### 7.1 Safe zones in a 1080×1920 frame
| Platform | Top | Bottom | Left | Right | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| YouTube Shorts (Google's universal vertical guide) | 288 | 672 | 48 | 192 | [S: AdKit measurement of Google's template, Sep 2026] |
| Instagram Reels (Meta percentages converted) | ~270 (14%) | ~672 (35%), ~770 (40%) on the right | ~65 (6%) | ~65, but the rightmost ~230 px in the lower area is buttons | [S: Hopper HQ] |
| **Box that works on both** | **288** | **672** | **65** | **230** | → content box x 65–850, y 288–1248 (≈785×960) [H] |

- The **Instagram profile grid** crops the cover to 3:4. Keep cover text and faces between y = 240 and 1680 [S: Hopper HQ].
- **Placement inside the box** [H]: hook text at y ≈ 300–600; captions at y ≈ 1000–1240; faces near the vertical centre with headroom.

### 7.2 Choosing about 15 Shorts from one stream
- **Self-contained test:** if a viewer needs the previous 15–20 seconds to follow the clip, it isn't a Short yet [S: vendor guides].
- **Four parts:** hook → enough context → development → payoff. Pay off by 30–45 seconds and end right after the punchline or lesson [S/H].
- **Reject** clips that depend on unseen context, misrepresent the speaker, duplicate another clip, or stop just before the answer [S].
- **Score candidates from 1 to 5** on hook strength, self-containment, payoff clarity, emotional peak and visual proof. Pick the top scorers under a mix quota [H]:
  - ~4 demos or "wow" results
  - ~4 practical lessons
  - ~3 fails or comedy
  - ~2 hot takes
  - ~2 mini stories
  Vendor guides also recommend mixing clip roles rather than ten versions of the same idea [S].
- **Faces:** wide framing is linked to lower virality in a TikTok study [S: arXiv 2111.02452]. Keep the face medium-close.

### 7.3 Hook formats and the first frame [H]
Hook formats:
1. **Result first:** show the finished thing, then "here's how".
2. **Start mid-action** on the peak line.
3. **A specific claim with numbers:** exact figures beat vague ones, as Galloway notes [S].
4. **A visual question:** an error on screen plus "why?".
5. **Contrarian claim.**
6. **"Watch this"** with an on-screen countdown.

First-frame rules:
- **Never black, never a fade-in.** The frame must carry a 4–8 word text hook inside the safe box that matches what is on screen.
- Third-party guides suggest about **75–80% viewed vs. swiped away** as healthy. YouTube publishes no benchmark, and Shorts view counting changed in March 2025 [S: Subscribr, Bytecap].

### 7.4 Cut density, loops and length
- **Visual change** (cut, punch, layout switch or graphic) roughly every 1–3 seconds. Hold proof shots longer. Typical high-energy Shorts run 20–40 cuts a minute [H].
- **Loops:**
  - Sentence loop: the last line flows into the first.
  - Visual loop: the end frame matches the opening composition.
  - Keep room tone or music continuous across the seam.
  - The loop must still make sense on the first viewing [S: Faceless.so and CapCut guides].
- **Length:** Shorts can run up to 3 minutes for uploads since 15 Oct 2024 [S: YouTube Help]. Stay at 30–60 seconds unless the story needs more.

### 7.5 Vertical layouts for screen + facecam [H, with S where marked]
- **Don't letterbox a full 16:9 desktop** into a 1080-wide frame. That's about 0.56× scale and text becomes unreadable. Crop a region of interest of about 960×860 px and scale it up to fill the width.
- **Webcam quality:** a 1080p webcam cropped to full 9:16 is about 608×1080 upscaled 1.78×, which looks soft. Prefer band layouts unless the camera shoots 4K.
- **Layout A, face on top (reaction-led):**
  - face band y 0–620, with the eyes at about y 380–450
  - screen band y 620–1560
  - the strip below y 1560 is blurred or brand fill, since UI covers it
  - big hook words sit on the seam
  - keep key UI out of the right 230 px of the lower screen band
- **Layout B, screen on top (proof-led):**
  - screen y 220–1080
  - face y 1080–1500
  - captions on the seam
- **Layout C, full face with a floating screenshot card:** for talking moments.
- **Switch layouts on story beats:** face full when emotional, screen full when showing proof. Ready-made top/bottom 70/30 and 50/50 templates exist [S: Tella docs, Remotion layout guide].

### 7.6 Audio and rights
- **YouTube:** any Short **over 1 minute with an active Content ID claim** (including manual claims) is blocked worldwide. Most songs can be used for up to 90 seconds in a 3-minute Short; some only for 60 or 30. YouTube Audio Library tracks don't get claims [S: YouTube Help].
- **Instagram:** Reels downloaded by viewers lose licensed audio; only original audio stays [S: Social Samosa].
- **Default mix:** for talking Shorts, trending audio sits well under the voice. When the trend *is* the joke, the audio leads and the voice is cut to it [H].

## 8. Wall checklists

**A. Before editing (director)**
- [ ] What is it about emotionally? Spine written as one verb phrase
- [ ] Title and thumbnail written; first 3 seconds match the thumbnail
- [ ] Frame plot: how framing and graphics change as stakes rise
- [ ] Beat list: each beat has a want, an obstacle and a turn; beats with no turn are cut or compressed
- [ ] Irony pairs found (claim → contradiction)
- [ ] Radio cut locked before any graphics
- [ ] Misconception stated before the explanation (explainers)

**B. Each cut**
- [ ] The emotion carries across the cut
- [ ] New progressive information arrives
- [ ] Cut on thought completion, a blink or breath, or movement
- [ ] Subject within ~10% of the previous position, or the eye is led to it
- [ ] Not two shots of the same size in a row
- [ ] The reaction follows its true stimulus

**C. Long-form structure**
- [ ] The first 30 seconds would work as a Short
- [ ] Re-engagement at ~25% and ~50% of runtime
- [ ] An open loop is always running
- [ ] Progress marker each chapter
- [ ] Nothing static for more than ~40 seconds
- [ ] Stakes stair-step upward
- [ ] Payoff last, then out within seconds
- [ ] Chapters valid: starts at 00:00, at least 3, each ≥10 s
- [ ] End-screen zone left clear

**D. Effects pass**
- [ ] Every effect has a trigger in the footage
- [ ] Hit on the transient frame or one frame before
- [ ] Noise-based shake with decay, on the footage layer only
- [ ] Budget respected
- [ ] The largest effect is on the largest moment
- [ ] ≤3 flashes in any second; no red strobe
- [ ] Text being read never shakes

**E. Stream → video**
- [ ] Logged chat spikes, loudness peaks, turns, proof frames
- [ ] Template chosen (A–E)
- [ ] Cold open, context bridge of 30 s or less, return to the cold open
- [ ] Greetings and chat-name reading cut; waiting compressed
- [ ] Inside jokes explained or cut
- [ ] Stream music replaced

**F. Shorts**
- [ ] Passes the 15–20 second context test
- [ ] Payoff by 30–45 seconds
- [ ] First frame has a text hook in the safe box
- [ ] Captions at y ≈ 1000–1240, clear of the right 230 px
- [ ] Layout chosen per beat
- [ ] Loop seam checked
- [ ] No Content ID claim on anything over 60 seconds
